# Point Multipliers Steer Effort — They Don't Create It. Use Sales to Rebalance Subjects, Not to Boost Output

## Pattern

In a point economy (XP, March Madness points, leaderboard scores), temporary multipliers — "flash sales," double-point days — reliably move *where* students spend effort, and reliably fail to increase *how much* effort exists. The strong use is therefore allocation, not acceleration:

1. **To pull students into avoided subjects:** "On Monday, each math lesson completed was worth double points, encouraging students who normally don't work on math… many students chose to work on the promoted subject for most of the day."
2. **Rotated by subject** so the whole week's mix rebalances, with the multiplier landing hardest where the app's structure rewards engagement (science "fire sales" topped the leaderboard because IXL's test-first structure paid fast).
3. **Never as a productivity lever:** the one controlled test in the corpus — doubling point values by subject with a control comparison — found "a negligible effect on productivity over time, while keeping points consistent, the control, seems to have gotten better results."

The mechanism: a multiplier changes relative prices inside the economy, so rational students reallocate — that's the whole effect. Total effort is governed by other things (targets, culture, momentum — the §3 patterns), which is why doubling everything moves nothing. A guide who understands the multiplier as a steering wheel gets subject balance cheaply; one who uses it as an accelerator dilutes the economy and gets noise.

Related: BL-084 defends subject balance conversationally; this is the economy-design lever for the same goal. BL-108 gates *how* points are earned; this governs *what they're worth, when*. BL-068's visibility rule and [Guide]'s leaderboard observation ("loses motivational effectiveness when not operationalized/consistent — regular, predictable access to standings") share the operational-consistency requirement.

## Evidence

*1- Rotated subject sales reallocating effort, as designed*

Weekly flash-sale rotation (math Monday, a different subject Tuesday–Thursday): "We noticed that many students chose to work on the promoted subject for most of the day or decided to spend their extra time on that particular subject."

Source artifact:[Guide Blog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/12DdoEWay9MCWxZgnaZh1JBDUb3wRu1aYpYaEQaF8U60/edit?usp=drive_link)

*2- The reallocation confirmed by a second guide, with the structural explanation*

"Occasionally during core skills, subjects go on sale and are worth double the amount of points. During the weeks with 'fire sales' science has come out on top" — traced to IXL's fail→learn→apply structure paying quickly under the multiplier.

Source artifact:[Guide Blog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1TuhYeuJqrtx9p2DDHfp5bdwtG8382qWBjHUSt4hAPs4/edit?usp=drive_link)

*3- The controlled test of the acceleration assumption*

An explicit experiment doubling March Madness point values by subject, charted against a constant-points control across weeks: doubling produced a ~3.7-point average *decrease* while the control improved — with the guide's own caveat that concurrent cultural changes may have influenced the data.

Source artifact:[Guide Blog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1wAbfMWnWeL4lP9rnVtcUOmtaXBGQ_3BvJabU5zr0ifE/edit?usp=drive_link)

## Anti-Pattern

The anti-pattern is deploying multipliers to chase total productivity — expecting doubled values to double effort.

*1- The in-corpus failed test*

[Guide]'s experiment is the anti-pattern executed and measured: the productivity-boost hypothesis, tested against a control, returned negative — "keeping points consistent… seems to have gotten better results."

Source artifact:[Guide Blog](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1wAbfMWnWeL4lP9rnVtcUOmtaXBGQ_3BvJabU5zr0ifE/edit?usp=drive_link)

*2- The economy-integrity parallel*

BL-084's anti-pattern (rewards negotiated on a single total) and BL-108's gaming evidence show the adjacent failure: when point values move for reasons students can arbitrage, the economy's connection to learning is what pays the price.

Source artifact: cross-reference BL-084, BL-108

## Reusable Coaching Move

Before announcing any sale or multiplier:

1. **Name the allocation goal:** which subject is under-served, by whom? (If the answer is "we just want more output," stop — use §3.4 momentum patterns instead.)
2. **Rotate it** so every subject gets its pull-day and no single app is permanently over-paid.
3. **Keep the base economy stable and visible** (BL-068, [Guide]'s consistency rule) — sales work against a predictable baseline.
4. **Check the mix, not the total,** the following week: the win condition is students touching avoided subjects, and the effect should be visible in subject distribution.

## Confidence: Medium — three instances from three guides including one controlled (if confounded) experiment, all one campus-year; the reallocation effect is consistently documented, the no-acceleration finding rests on a single small test.
