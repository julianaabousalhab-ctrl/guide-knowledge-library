# Take the Project to the Need — Source It From the Community and Keep Students in Direct Contact With the People It Serves

## Pattern

Community-impact projects run differently when the need is discovered *from the community* and students stay in repeated, physical contact with the people the project serves. The converged design across guides:

1. **Source the need through direct discovery** — students interview the public, visit the site, or vote among real local options before the project is defined, rather than receiving a guide-chosen cause.
2. **Keep the contact repeated, not ceremonial** — return visits during the work ("gone twice to help with donating and see the need since the start of the session"), live partner conversations, and post-project contact (thank-you letters to supporters), so the beneficiaries stay people rather than becoming an abstraction.
3. **Let the community formalize the relationship** — signatures, partnership approvals from the real organizations, metrics the partner can verify.

The mechanism is distinct from BL-110's real client: a client judges the *work product*, supplying professional constraints and revision pressure; beneficiary contact supplies *witnessed need*, which drives ownership and persistence. The tracker data shows the ownership/impact/transformation dimensions rising across weeks as contact accumulates — and the guide who most explicitly runs this design is the same guide whose year-end retrospective diagnosed its absence.

## Anti-Pattern

The anti-pattern is the walled project — a community cause worked entirely from inside the classroom, with the beneficiaries an abstraction — and its cost is documented in the guides' own retrospectives.

*The diagnosis, from the guide who then built the fix*

> "When learning stayed inside the walls of school, students struggled to see school as more exciting or meaningful than vacation."

And the single-student version, with the count that matters: "this school year only one workshop in wonder lab was done off-campus" — recorded as the root cause for a student who, through Session 5, still chose vacation over school.

## Reusable Coaching Move

Use the Discover → Meet → Return → Formalize process when planning any community-facing project.

### 1. Discover the need in the field

Open with student-run discovery in the real community — street interviews, site visits, or a vote among locally researched options. The project's subject should be something students heard or saw, not received.

### 2. Meet the beneficiaries early

Before the main effort begins, put students physically in front of the people or place the project serves. The visit converts the cause from concept to faces.

### 3. Return during the work

Schedule at least one mid-project return — delivering, helping, measuring — so the need stays live while effort is hardest. Close the loop after: thank-yous and report-backs keep the relationship real past the fundraiser.

### 4. Formalize with the community

Get the external side on record — signatures, a partner's approval of the plan, an impact metric the partner verifies (pairs with BL-110 where the partner also judges work product, and with BL-092's session-long installation logic).

The coaching standard is not:

> "Students ran a project for a good cause and hit their fundraising goal."

The standard is:

> "Students chose the cause because they went and saw it, met the people it serves more than once, and the community partner — not the guide — confirmed the need was real and the impact happened."

## Confidence: Medium-High — eight guides across roughly seven campuses (Austin MS, Brownsville, Lake Forest/OC, two New York projects, Santa Barbara, and two more) executed the design in the same session, with rising tracker scores across weeks, named step variants converging independently (street interviews, heartbreak-list votes, rapport-first site visits), and the anti-pattern documented in two guides' own retrospectives including a within-guide diagnosis-to-execution arc (Taylor Sweeney). Still capped below High because the tracker evidence is guide-authored weekly summaries with rubric self-scores on a template that rewards need-visibility (the codified craft is the contact design the rubric doesn't prescribe), and 9 of the 58 tracker screenshots were read in depth — no stalled-project counter-case has surfaced yet, which is the one contrast that would either bound or fully confirm the mechanism.
