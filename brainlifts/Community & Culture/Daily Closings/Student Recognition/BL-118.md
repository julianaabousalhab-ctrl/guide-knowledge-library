# Teach the Shout-Out as a Form — Model It, Give Every Student a Turn, and Upgrade Vague Praise Live

## Pattern

The Closing rubric sets the bar — specific, behavior-based, life-skill-aligned, student-given shout-outs — but the bar doesn't teach itself. Left alone, peer recognition reliably drifts vague, outcome-based, or friend-based. The guides whose closings meet and exceed the bar converge on treating the shout-out as a *teachable form*, coached like any other skill:

1. **Model the anatomy explicitly** — name the action observed, describe what happened, share the impact — with worked examples before students try.
2. **Guarantee universal turns through low-stakes structures** — passing games, songs, spinners that route a turn to every student, so recognition isn't monopolized by the confident few.
3. **Upgrade vague praise in the moment** — when a shout-out lands generic, the guide (or increasingly, peers) coaches it up live: attach the behavior, the evidence, the life skill.
4. **Have students define the standard themselves** — reflecting on what a strong shout-out looks like, so the form transfers from guide scaffold to student norm.

This is the peer-recognition counterpart of BL-026/BL-008 (which govern the *guide's* recognition craft): here the student is the recognizer, and the guide's move is teaching, structuring, and upgrading.

## Anti-Pattern

The anti-pattern is running the shout-out slot without teaching the form — recognition happens, participation looks healthy, and the content quietly degrades into outcomes, generalities, and friendship.

*The three documented drift modes, from closings QC'd in the same corpus*

> "Shout-outs were often outcome-based and lacked clear specificity around the behaviors or core life [skills]." 

> "Some shout-outs drifted toward friend-based recognition rather than behavior." (Learning Lab, Week 7)

## Reusable Coaching Move

Use the Model → Structure → Upgrade → Transfer process in every Closing's recognition slot.

### 1. Model the anatomy

Before students give shout-outs, show one: the action observed, what happened, the impact or life skill it demonstrated. Re-model whenever the form drifts.

### 2. Structure universal turns

Use a low-stakes access mechanism (passing game, spinner, rotation) so every student both gives and receives; keep prompts ready for students who freeze, and treat a reluctant student's first shout-out as a win worth its own recognition.

### 3. Upgrade live, warmly

When praise lands generic, coach it up on the spot — "what did they *do*?", "which life skill is that?" — and invite peers to do the upgrading as the norm matures.

### 4. Transfer the standard

Periodically have students define and self-assess what strong shout-outs look like, shifting the specificity bar from guide scaffold to community norm (this is the recognition-slot complement of BL-111's student-run closing).

The coaching standard is not:

> "Students gave the shout-outs and everyone participated positively."

The standard is:

> "Every student had a turn, each recognition named a behavior and its life skill — and when one came out vague, someone (increasingly a student) upgraded it on the spot."

## Confidence: Medium-High — four guides at four campuses and levels (Miami L2, Brownsville L2, Austin Wonder Lab, an expansion campus) independently ran the teach-and-upgrade craft, with the drift modes documented as anti-patterns in the same corpus's QC records (including one guide's own note); capped because the specificity bar itself is rubric-mandated (the codified craft is only the how — model, universal turns, live upgrade, student-defined standard), several evidence rows are AI-QC summaries of recordings rather than guide narration, and no longitudinal data yet shows the scaffold successfully fading into a student-held norm.
