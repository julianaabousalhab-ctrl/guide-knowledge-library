# Design the 360 So Specificity Is Structural — Balanced, Required Comments Beat Deficit-Triggered Checkboxes

## Pattern

The quality of 360 peer-feedback data is set by the form's prompt architecture before a single student answers. Five campuses ran the same exercise (peers assessing core life skills) with five different instrument designs, and the response data ranks the design choices:

1. **Require a balanced comment for every student reviewed** — "what the student is doing well *and* where they can improve" — rather than triggering a comment only on a deficit (an unchecked skill or a "Not Yet"). Deficit-triggered prompts read as accusation paperwork and get evaded; balanced-required prompts read as the task itself and get answered.
2. **Decompose skills into observable behavior questions** ("Does Logan treat classmates [with respect]...", "Does this student stay on task without being reminded?") rather than skill-label checkboxes — the BL-073/BL-106 observability principle applied to the peer instrument. When students do answer behavior-level items, the answers come back datestamp-specific ("The only time he didn't stay focused was at 9/23/2025 9:17 AM playing Bro-Mon.").
3. **Expect and engineer against evasion** — mandatory text fields without a real prompt produce ritual compliance ("." / "nape"), which silently poisons downstream use of the data.

The instrument feeds everything downstream: BL-116's divergence investigations, culture diagnoses (level models justified by "360 feedback showed a need"), and coaching conversations that review peer results with the student. Bad prompt architecture starves all of them.

## Evidence

*1 — The balanced-required design, and its measured output*

The form asks, per student reviewed: "Please provide details and/or feedback on what the student is doing well and where they can improve" — a required, balanced comment for every peer. Output: **577 free-text comments, 1% trivial/evasive, average 168 characters** of substantive, behavior-referenced feedback ("[Student] works hard on skills but sometimes she doesn't work independently at all, and will go on other websites during core skills.").

*2 — Behavior-level decomposition producing observation-grade answers*

The BTX form decomposes each skill into observable behavior items per student ("Does Logan show the following behaviors under Teamwork?" — three sub-behaviors, Yes/Not yet) with a comment gate on Not Yet: "write one kind, specific, and helpful [suggestion]." Where students engaged, answers carried direct observations with dates and specifics.

*3 — The instrument's data feeding real coaching downstream*

The corpus's peer results are the same data guides elsewhere used with students — the documented case of a student processing "Two students out of 25 voted that she was not always kind" into calibrated self-reflection, and level models justified by "360 feedback showed a need for improving scores in all areas." A well-designed instrument is what makes those uses legitimate.

## Anti-Pattern

The anti-pattern is deficit-triggered or gate-free designs — comments requested only when something negative was marked, or required fields with no substantive prompt — and the evasion is measurable in the same corpus:

- **Deficit-triggered prompts yield sparse, thin coverage:** the ATX L2 form asks for an explanation only "If you didn't check one (or more) of the core life skills" — across 132 responses it drew ~1.6 comments per respondent averaging 56 characters ("aleina could improve her independent some times"), versus ~17 per respondent averaging 168 characters under the balanced-required design. The conditional prompt produces feedback only where a deficit was marked, so most students receive no written peer feedback at all.
- **Mandatory fields answered with ritual tokens:** BTX's Not-Yet comment gates drew "." and copy-paste filler in **44% of text answers**; the Nova 3-6 form's fields came back **89% trivial** ("nape").
- **Recognition-only designs capping depth:** Miami L3's "ONLY check the life skill(s) the student is exemplifying" plus brief-detail field produced **37% trivial** responses averaging 40 characters — and the non-trivial remainder drifting into unstructured grievances ("The student should stop being mean to me...") that a behavior-anchored prompt would have channeled.

## Reusable Coaching Move

Use the Balance → Behaviors → Gate → Audit process when building (or fixing) a 360 instrument.

### 1. Balance the prompt

Ask for strength *and* growth for every student reviewed, unconditionally. Never make the comment field a consequence of marking a deficit — that design teaches students the cheapest path is marking nothing.

### 2. Ask about behaviors, not labels

Phrase items as observable behaviors a peer could have seen this week ("stays on task without being reminded"), mirroring the "looks like" discipline of BL-073 and the decomposition of BL-106. Labels invite vibes; behaviors invite evidence.

### 3. Gate quality, not just presence

A required field is not a quality gate. Pair Not-Yet answers with the kind-specific-helpful frame students already know (BL-118's shout-out anatomy, BL-052's observation-plus-impact bar), and have guides model one strong written example before the survey opens.

### 4. Audit the responses before using them

Before results feed coaching (BL-116) or culture diagnosis, scan for evasion rates and token answers. A 360 with 44% "." answers isn't measuring the community — it's measuring the form. Fix the instrument before re-running, and treat evasive cohorts as a data-collection redo, not a student verdict.

The coaching standard is not:

> "Every student completed the 360 and all required fields were filled."

The standard is:

> "Every student reviewed got a balanced, behavior-referenced comment — and the response set passes an evasion audit before anyone coaches from it."

## Confidence: Medium — the design contrast is empirical and cross-campus (five instruments, quantified: balanced-required drew ~17 substantive comments per respondent at 1% evasion; deficit-triggered drew ~1.6 per respondent at 56-char depth; recognition-only and gate-free designs ran 37–89% trivial), with the strongest comparison age-matched (ATX MS vs. Miami L3). Capped because each instrument is a single-session snapshot from a different campus (design and campus/age are partially confounded — Nova's 89% is 3rd–6th graders), form authorship is inferred from campus rather than documented, and no within-campus before/after redesign exists yet — one campus rerunning its 360 with a balanced-required prompt and showing the volume and evasion shift would settle causality and raise this to High.
