# Ask the Level-Fit Question Directly in Session 1 — Treat Every "No" as a Placement-Verification Trigger

## Pattern

Early in the session, ask each student point-blank whether they are learning at the right level — "not too hard, not too easy" — by survey or in a 1:1. When students are asked directly, roughly three in ten say no in Session 1: far more than surface through complaints, dashboards, or guide noticing alone.

The Guide behavior has three steps:

1. **Ask directly, early.** The blunt question, asked of every student in the first weeks — not only of students already showing problems.
2. **Collect the direction and the location.** A "No" is followed by which way (too hard / too easy) and where (which subject, which app) — the free text in the corpus shows students name both when asked.
3. **Verify placement before coaching effort.** Each "No" triggers a placement check — watch the student work the actual task (BL-032) and separate fit from strategy and system causes (BL-041) — rather than a motivation response.

The mechanism: wrong-level work corrodes exactly the metrics guides coach hardest — motivation, XP, app engagement — while masquerading as an effort problem. The direct question converts a hidden design failure into a named, checkable claim, at the cost of one survey item.

Related: This adds the self-report channel to BL-096's data channel (fixed-cadence numeric flags) — the two catch different students: BL-096 finds drift the student hasn't voiced; this finds mismatch the dashboard hasn't shown yet. Verification runs through BL-032 and BL-041.

## Evidence

*1- Asked directly, ~30% of Session 1 students report wrong-level placement*

"Do you feel you are learning at the right level — not too hard, not too easy?" draws 30–32% "No" in Session 1 across three near-independent multi-campus datasets (n=362, n=340, n=296 after deduplication). Later-session cohorts (different campuses, so not a trend claim) still run 18–22% — a substantial minority in every cohort measured.

## Anti-Pattern

The anti-pattern is reading a wrong-level student's low XP and fading effort as a motivation gap — and reaching for the motivational toolkit (incentives, trackers, pep talks) before checking fit.

*1- The masquerade measured, within this corpus*

Students who report being at the wrong level are 2.4× more likely to also report not feeling motivated (31% motivated-"No" vs 13% for right-level students; n=526 vs 1,809) and 2.2× more likely to say they cannot get unstuck without Guide help (28% vs 13%). Level misfit and apparent motivation/independence problems travel together — which is exactly what makes the misdiagnosis easy. (Association, not proven causation; the direction is corroborated by the placement-resolution evidence above.)

*2- The documented resolution, from the library (cross-corpus)*

BL-061's evidence records the shape of the mistake avoided: a student's reading XP problems ended "once we placed her in the proper grade level" — the visible symptom was an XP shortfall, and the fix was placement, not a motivational lever.

This is the same misdiagnosis mechanism BL-071 documents for unclear pass criteria: a design failure presenting as a student failure. A third of a Session 1 room may be sitting in it silently; not asking is what makes it invisible.

Note: the survey corpus contains no recorded instance of a guide actually applying a motivational fix to a placement problem — the within-corpus evidence is the correlational masquerade pattern; the instance-level evidence comes from the library's placement-resolution cases.

## Reusable Coaching Move

Run the Level-Fit Sweep in the first weeks of every session:

1. **Ask everyone:** "Are you learning at the right level — not too hard, not too easy?" (survey item or 1:1 opener).
2. **For every No:** "Which way — and in which subject or app?"
3. **Verify before coaching:** watch the student work that task (BL-032); classify the barrier — fit, strategy, or system (BL-041).
4. **Act on fit:** placement test, level change, or app change — then re-ask the question after the change lands.
5. **Expect volume:** ~1 in 3 "No" in Session 1 is normal, not alarming. The alarming version is a room where nobody was asked.

## Confidence: Medium — a large, replicated point estimate (three datasets, multi-campus) plus directional free text; the ask-then-verify routine's effectiveness is corroborated by library evidence (BL-061) rather than demonstrated within the survey corpus itself.
